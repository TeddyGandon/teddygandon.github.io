Every team I've led has been a small negotiation of habits. Not conflict — negotiation. The engineer who states disagreement flatly in a stand-up isn't being difficult; in their working culture, hedging *would* be the confusing move. The one who agrees to everything in the meeting and raises concerns a day later by message isn't avoiding the topic; they're giving themselves — and you — room to reconsider without an audience.

None of this is exotic. It's the ordinary texture of multicultural teams, and most of management is learning to read it correctly instead of translating it into your own defaults and getting the wrong answer.

## What actually transfers

A few things have held up across every team, regardless of where people were from:

- **Say the same thing twice, differently.** Once in the meeting, once in writing. Not because people weren't listening, but because "what was decided" and "what was heard" drift, and the drift is where culture hides.
- **Ask for the disagreement you didn't get.** Silence in a review is not consensus. It is, more often, someone waiting for a more private channel.
- **Separate the plan from the person.** Feedback on an approach reads very differently depending on whether the team's norm is directness-as-respect or directness-as-attack. The content can stay the same; the wrapping has to change.

## The part that doesn't get easier

Time zones and holidays are the visible cost. The invisible one is slower: building enough shared context that a two-line Slack message is enough, instead of a paragraph of caveats. That only comes from repetition — the same kinds of decisions, made the same way, often enough that the team stops needing the caveats.

Twenty years in, the lesson hasn't changed much: multicultural management isn't a special skill bolted onto engineering management. It's engineering management done carefully, in an environment that punishes assumptions faster than a homogeneous one would.
