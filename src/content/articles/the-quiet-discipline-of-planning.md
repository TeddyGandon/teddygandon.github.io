Nobody joins engineering to become good at planning. It's the least glamorous skill on the list, and it's the one that determines whether the interesting work gets to happen at all.

## Planning is a form of respect

A plan that accounts for the unglamorous realities — someone's on leave, this dependency is owned by another team, that migration always takes longer than it looks — is a form of respect for the people who have to execute it. A plan that ignores those realities isn't optimistic, it's just wrong, and the team pays for the gap between the plan and reality in the worst way: quietly, in overtime and eroded trust.

## A few habits that hold up

- **Plan in ranges, not points.** A single date invites false confidence. A range invites the right conversation about what would move it.
- **Leave room for the thing you haven't found yet.** Every non-trivial project has one. Budgeting for it explicitly beats discovering it has eaten your buffer.
- **Revisit the plan on a cadence, not just when it breaks.** By the time a plan visibly breaks, the useful moment to adjust it has usually passed.

## The payoff

Good planning doesn't feel like anything when it's working — that's the point. Nobody notices the release that shipped on the date everyone expected. They notice the one that didn't. Twenty years in, that's still the quiet argument for taking planning seriously: it's the discipline that lets the actually interesting engineering work stay interesting, instead of turning into a scramble.
