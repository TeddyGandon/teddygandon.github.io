When I walked into Brototype in Kochi, India, the atmosphere immediately reminded me of my own early days as an engineer. The bootcamp thrives on peer-driven, hands-on learning, and the energy of young engineers tackling real-world problems was contagious. I had the privilege of conducting two talks there, focusing on problem-solving methods and the importance of feedback loops. What struck me most was how deeply this approach resonated with my own journey.

I have always believed that the best engineers are forged not in classrooms but in the crucible of real challenges. Early in my career, I was fortunate to have mentors who gave me the freedom to experiment, fail, and try again. Their guidance was not about handing me answers but about helping me build the resilience to find them myself. Those experiences shaped my philosophy: growth comes from solving problems, reflecting on the process, and receiving honest feedback. It is a cycle that mirrors the iterative nature of engineering itself.

At Brototype, I saw that same cycle in action. Students were not passively absorbing theory; they were building, breaking, and rebuilding. They were learning to ask the right questions, to test assumptions, and to embrace the discomfort of not knowing. This reminded me of Kolb’s experiential learning theory, which emphasizes that knowledge is created through experience and reflection. It also echoed Carol Dweck’s work on growth mindset, which shows that those who view failure as a learning opportunity are more likely to succeed in the long run. These ideas are not abstract; they are lived realities in environments like Brototype.

The feedback loop is what makes this process sustainable. In engineering, feedback is the mechanism by which systems improve. In human development, it is the same. I remember countless moments when a mentor’s candid observation changed the way I approached a problem. Sometimes the feedback was tough to hear, but it was always invaluable. At Brototype, I encouraged participants to seek feedback relentlessly, not just from mentors but from peers. Feedback is not about judgment; it is about clarity. It is the compass that keeps growth on course.

As I spoke to the engineers in Kochi, I felt a deep sense of continuity. The spark that my mentors once gave me is now something I can pass on. Supporting the next generation is not just about teaching technical skills; it is about instilling a mindset of curiosity, resilience, and collaboration. It is about helping them see that failure is not the end but the beginning of learning. It is about showing them that engineering is not just about code, but about systems, people, and the courage to keep improving.

Leaving Brototype, I carried with me a renewed conviction. The future of engineering will be built by those who embrace problems, who welcome feedback, and who never stop learning. My role, and the role of every experienced engineer, is to nurture that spirit. For those who come after, the path is clear: keep solving, keep listening, and keep growing. The spark lives on when we pass it forward.

For those who come after ✨
