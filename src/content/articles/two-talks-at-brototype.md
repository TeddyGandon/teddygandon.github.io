I gave two talks at Brototype, a coding bootcamp in Kochi, India, on problem-solving methods and the feedback loop. Different rooms, same underlying question from the audience, asked in different words each time: how do I go from "I can follow a tutorial" to "I can solve a problem I haven't seen before"?

## Problem solving is a method, not a talent

The instinct, early on, is to treat debugging and problem solving as something you either have or don't. It isn't. It's a small, repeatable method: state the problem precisely before touching the fix, form a hypothesis, test the narrowest thing that would prove or disprove it, and resist the urge to change five things at once because one of them might work. Bootcamp students are usually fluent in syntax and unpracticed in this method — which is exactly the gap the talks were aimed at.

## The feedback loop is the actual curriculum

The second talk was really an extension of the first: none of the above matters without a tight feedback loop. Write code, get a signal — a failing test, a reviewer's comment, a production error — and close the gap between the mistake and the correction as fast as possible. Long loops are where bad habits calcify undetected; short loops are where they get caught early enough to still be cheap to fix. Most of what separates a junior developer six months in from one two years in isn't raw skill — it's how many times they've been through that loop, and how honestly.

## What stayed with me

Standing in front of a room of people just starting out is a good way to notice which parts of the job you've stopped explaining to yourself, because you no longer question them. Talking through problem-solving methods and feedback loops for two audiences in Kochi was a reminder that the fundamentals don't get less important as a career goes on — they just stop being visible, buried under twenty years of doing them automatically.
