"Multicultural management" shows up on a lot of profiles, mine included, usually as a single line next to "20 years of engineering." It sounds like a value statement. But in practice, it's a set of recurring, concrete frictions that show up in specific meetings, on specific days, and that either get handled or quietly compound into attrition. After a decade of leading teams spread across countries, time zones, and working styles, here's what it actually looks like when it's working.

## Silence means different things

The first friction is the most deceptive because it doesn't look like a problem. You ask a question in a meeting. Some people answer immediately. Others say nothing. The instinct (especially for a manager trained in a low-context, say-what-you-mean culture) is to read silence as absence: no objection, no idea, nothing to add. That reading is wrong often enough to be dangerous.

In several of the teams I've run, silence in the room reliably meant "I disagree, but not here." The disagreement was real; it just wasn't going to surface in a synchronous, semi-public setting. Cultures differ in whether disagreement is expected to be voiced directly and openly, or surfaced privately and diplomatically (1). Neither is a lesser communication style. Treating one as the default and the other as a deficiency is how you end up making a decision the room never actually agreed with; you just couldn't hear the disagreement over your own assumptions.

The fix isn't "encourage everyone to speak up," which mostly rewards the people already comfortable doing so. The fix is to build a second channel (async written feedback, a 1:1 the next day, an explicit "tell me the version of this you didn't say in the meeting") and treating what comes back through it as equally valid input, not a side conversation.

Silence has a noisy mirror-image worth naming too. In Anglo-Saxon, and especially American, workplaces, "amazing" functions closer to punctuation than to an assessment: it greets a rough first draft, a mediocre demo, and a genuinely excellent piece of work with the same word, distinguished only by a tone that's easy to miss if it isn't your first language or your default culture. A manager who takes it at face value will misjudge how something actually landed almost as badly as one who reads silence as consent. Different failure mode, same root cause: the words carry less objective signal than the culture-specific decoding they require.

## Hierarchy is a working assumption and NOT a personality trait

The second friction: who feels entitled to push back on whom. In some teams I've managed, a junior engineer will correct a senior's design in front of everyone, mid-meeting, without a second thought. In others, that same correction will only ever reach me privately, weeks later, framed as a question rather than a challenge, because openly contradicting someone more senior reads as disrespectful, not as rigor (2).

In fact, this isn't a confidence gap you coach away but a working assumption people bring from years of professional norms before they ever joined your team, and it doesn't dissolve because the org chart is flat on paper. What changes it, slowly, is the manager visibly modeling the behavior they want: publicly inviting the junior engineer's objection, crediting it by name, and doing that repeatedly enough that it stops being a special occasion. This isn't the "encourage everyone to speak up" fix I waved off in the previous section, that one broadcasts a generic ask into a room and hopes. Modeling is targeted at one specific person, repeated, and credited publicly; it's closer to building the second channel than to the poster-on-the-wall version of open expression, which is exactly why it can earn trust that a blanket invitation can't. Psychological safety is demonstrated, once, in a moment that actually had stakes, and then again, and again, until people update their model of what's safe here. And no, **psychological safety isn't declared in a kickoff deck**.

## Async isn't a fallback

With teams spread across time zones, "we'll sync on it" is often a manager's way of avoiding the harder work of writing clearly. But when a decision only lives in a meeting that half the team was asleep for, you haven't made a decision: you've made a decision for the people in the room, and a rumor for everyone else.

The teams that have worked best for me treated async-first documentation as the primary artifact, and meetings as a lower-bandwidth confirmation of something already written down. That has a side benefit beyond time zones: it forces the same clarity discipline as writing a spec or an API contract. If the reasoning doesn't survive being written down plainly, it probably wasn't as settled as the meeting made it feel.

None of that makes a live conversation obsolete. When a written decision lands wrong, when a clipped sentence reads as a personal jab, or a nuance gets flattened across languages, a five-minute 1:1 clears up what could otherwise take three confused comment threads to untangle. The distinction that matters is scope: a 1:1 is the right tool for repairing one person's misreading, not the venue where a squad-level decision actually gets validated. Use it to fix understanding, not to relitigate the decision itself.

## What actually scales

None of this is solved by a values statement or a "we celebrate diversity" slide. It's solved by specific, boring mechanisms: a written channel for dissent that doesn't route through the loudest voice in the room, a manager who spends visible credibility inviting challenge from the people least likely to offer it unprompted, and documentation treated as the source of truth rather than the meeting notes nobody reads.

The throughline with everything else I've written about management is the same: the parts of leadership that feel like "soft skills" are usually just systems nobody has designed on purpose yet. Culture is one of the inputs to that system, not an exception to it.
