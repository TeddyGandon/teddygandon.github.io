In my intercultural management journey, I've faced the same challenge every time I've joined a new team: proving I'm actually fit to lead it. What's changed, every single time, is what counts as proof. Each culture I've worked with has its own answer test, built up over a much longer history than the three months I usually have to earn it, and none of them advertise the rules up front.

## Same thing, different question

Early on after joining a team, there's always one question underneath all the polite onboarding ones: are you actually someone I should follow. It's the same test everywhere, in the sense that every team runs it, usually without ever naming it out loud. It is not the same test twice, in the sense that what passes it is completely different in every culture I've managed in.

I learned this the expensive way the first time I moved into a new market. I answered the unspoken question the way I'd always answered it, assuming the proof that had worked on the last team would work on this one too, and watched it land flat. And not in an hostile way, just unconvincing. It took me a few weeks of that flat reception to realize the test itself had changed because I was managing a new culture.

Intercultural management is, at its core, about proving you have what it takes to lead **and** about knowing exactly where your own default idea of proof diverges from theirs.

## Leadership proofs by examples

The test question tends to arrive early, usually inside the first real piece of work, not in a welcome meeting. Nobody schedules it. You just notice, in hindsight, that the first real interaction was the test, and that how you handled it decided more about your standing than anything you said in the following month.

### France

In France, the proof is experience: what you've actually built, broken, and fixed, told in enough specific detail that it couldn't be borrowed from someone else's story. Vague competence doesn't register. A precise, slightly embarrassing account of the outage you caused and the fix you shipped registers immediately, because it can't be faked convincingly and everyone in the room knows it.

It isn't a coincidence. France has a centuries-old tradition, the *Compagnonnage*, in which a craftsperson's mastery is proven not by a diploma but by years of itinerant, hands-on work across different workshops, culminating in a "masterwork" judged by peers who did the same journey themselves; it's old enough and significant enough to be recognized by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage (1). The expectation I still meet in French teams is a direct descendant of that lineage: show me the thing you actually did, not the institution that certified you could. A manager who leads with a title instead of a story is, in that context, quietly assumed to not have much of a story to tell.

### Poland

In Poland, the proof is pragmatism: can you solve the actual problem in front of us, right now, without turning it into a longer conversation than it needs to be. When I started managing the team there, the first thing I was handed wasn't a roadmap review or an introductory strategy deck, it was a tricky latency issue the team had been circling for weeks. Passing that test meant getting hands-on with it immediately, tracing the actual bottleneck alongside them, not scheduling a discovery workshop about it or delegating it back down with a deadline attached.

That instinct shows up in the research too: Poland scores notably high on Hofstede's masculinity dimension, the axis that captures how much a culture rewards competition, decisiveness, and measurable results over consensus-building and process for its own sake (2). Pragmatism isn't a personality quirk of the specific teams I've worked with there. It's a fairly consistent cultural default, and it means the fastest way to lose credibility with a Polish team is to answer a concrete technical problem with a meeting.

### India

In India, the proof is the degree. "What's your degree" isn't small talk, it's often the actual leadership test, asked early, directly, and taken completely seriously, in a way that can catch a Western manager off guard the first time it happens mid-conversation rather than on a form.

Part of why is sheer numbers: the market produces vastly more engineering graduates than there are matching roles, and a formal credential becomes one of the few legible filters available at scale when everyone in the room is otherwise a stranger with an unverifiable resume (3). Part of it starts earlier than the job market: school systems that sort students by exam performance from a young age teach, early and repeatedly, that a credential is not decoration but the permanent record of where you ranked against everyone else who took the same exam.

## Intercultural management is all about embracing cultures

None of these is the right or the wrong in asking you to prove yourself. Each is rooted in its own history, and in how people in that culture have always had to prove themselves in the workplace, long before they were asking me to prove myself. Trying to import my own default proof into a culture that doesn't run on it doesn't read as confidence, it reads as not having done the homework, and it costs exactly the credibility it was supposed to build.

Because at the end of it, proving myself as a leader means going through the same door everyone else in that culture has always had to go through to prove themselves.

And that's why I love this job: I am having every single time the same journey as they are having.

**And every single time it's a brand new journey.**

## A simple footnote, as a reminder

Understanding the test (and sometimes passing it) is not only a proof of your leadership: **it's also a proof of respect.**
